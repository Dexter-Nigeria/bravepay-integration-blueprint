BravePay · plain-language briefing

How BravePay moves money

Three organisations have to cooperate before a customer can send ₦5,000 to a friend at another bank. Here is what each one does, in ordinary words.

For the BravePay team · 3 September 2026 · no technical background needed

The short version

BravePay is the app people see and touch. BankOne is the banking engine underneath it that actually holds accounts and moves money. NIBSS is the national system that carries money between different banks. BravePay talks to BankOne; BankOne talks to NIBSS; NIBSS reaches every other bank in the country.

That is the whole arrangement. Everything below explains it in more detail.

Who does what

BravePay

The shop

What customers see. The app, the login, the invoices, payroll, expense tracking, notifications, support. It knows who the customer is and what they want to do.

BankOne

The vault and the books

The licensed banking engine. It holds the real accounts, the real balances, and the official record of every naira. It also produces the reports the regulator requires.

NIBSS

The road between banks

The national infrastructure every Nigerian bank connects to. When money leaves one bank for another, it travels on NIBSS.

A useful way to hold it in your head: BravePay is the shopfront, BankOne is the back office, NIBSS is the road network. A customer never sees BankOne or NIBSS, in the same way you never see the warehouse behind a shop, or the roads a delivery van uses.

Why not just build the banking part ourselves?

Because holding customer deposits is a licensed, examined activity. A regulator will ask to see a proper double-entry ledger, an audit trail, and monthly returns filed on time. BankOne already has all of that, built for Nigerian microfinance banks. Building it ourselves would take years and would have to be defended to the CBN. Renting it is faster, cheaper and safer.

How BankOne connects to NIBSS

This is the part people find surprising: BravePay has no connection to NIBSS at all, and does not need one.

Getting your own direct connection to NIBSS is a serious undertaking. You need certification, your own bank code, a settlement bank holding money as security, and technical infrastructure that runs to a banking standard. It takes months and costs a great deal.

BankOne already has all of this. Hundreds of financial institutions sit behind BankOne's single connection. When BravePay sends a transfer instruction to BankOne, BankOne puts it onto NIBSS on our behalf. We inherit the connection rather than building one.

How BravePay connects to BankOne

Over the internet, using the same basic technology as a website.

When BravePay needs something from BankOne — check a balance, open an account, send a transfer — it sends a message over the internet to a BankOne address, and BankOne sends a message back. That exchange is called an API, and it travels over HTTP, the same protocol your browser uses to load a web page.

Think of it as writing to BankOne and getting a reply, thousands of times a day, in fractions of a second.

What travels along that link

  • "Open an account for this customer" → BankOne replies with a real account number.
  • "What is the balance on this account?" → BankOne replies with the figure.
  • "Whose account number is 0123456789 at GTBank?" → BankOne replies with the name.
  • "Send ₦5,000 from this account to that one" → BankOne does it and replies with the outcome.

The important detail: HTTP versus HTTPS

Those two extra letters matter more than anything else in this document.

✕ HTTP — a postcard

  • Anyone who handles it along the way can read every word.
  • That includes customer names, dates of birth, BVNs, account numbers.
  • It can also include the password our systems use to prove who they are.

✓ HTTPS — a sealed envelope

  • Scrambled from end to end. Handlers can carry it but cannot read it.
  • This is what every bank, and every serious website, uses.
  • The S stands for secure, and it is the difference.

BankOne's published documentation lists its live addresses as HTTP — the postcard. Out of the addresses printed in their guide, 94 are HTTP and only one is HTTPS. On top of that, one half of their system carries our password inside the web address itself, which is roughly like writing the key to the safe on the outside of the envelope: every system that handles the message keeps a copy in its records without anyone intending it.

What we are doing about this

This is a written question for Qore, BankOne's owner, and it must be answered before we go live. Most likely their documentation is simply out of date and the secure version works fine — that is common. But "probably fine" is not good enough for customer data, and it needs to be confirmed in writing rather than assumed.

Two reasons this is not optional. The CBN's cybersecurity rules for institutions like ours have applied since January 2023. And from 1 January 2027, Nigerian payment data must be stored and handled inside Nigeria — a rule that names microfinance banks specifically. Sending customer records around on postcards sits badly against both.

Meanwhile, our own side is built so that all traffic to BankOne leaves through a single controlled exit point. That gives us one place to enforce the secure version, one place to make sure passwords are never written into our logs, and one place to shut off if anything looks wrong.

Sending money to another bank

Here is what actually happens when a customer sends ₦5,000 to a friend at GTBank. It takes a few seconds.

  1. The customer enters the details

    Account number, bank, amount. So far nothing has left the app.

  2. We check who owns that account

    BravePay asks BankOne, BankOne asks NIBSS, NIBSS asks GTBank. The real account name comes back.

  3. The customer confirms the name

    They see the name on screen and check it is who they meant. This step prevents most money-sent-to-the-wrong-person disputes.

  4. We write down what they asked for

    Before anything moves, BravePay records the instruction. This sounds unremarkable and is the single most important safety step — see below.

  5. We instruct BankOne to send

    BankOne debits the customer's account and places the transfer onto NIBSS.

  6. NIBSS delivers it

    GTBank credits the friend's account, usually within seconds.

  7. We confirm and notify

    Once BankOne confirms it went through, the app updates and the customer gets a notification.

Why step 4 matters so much

Sometimes we send an instruction and no reply comes back — a network drops, a system is slow. We are then in the worst position in banking: we do not know whether the money moved.

Because we wrote the instruction down first, we can go back and ask BankOne "what happened to this one?" until we get a definite answer. Without that record, the instruction would simply vanish, and a customer would be left either missing money or wondering whether to send it again.

The rule that follows: we never give the money back to the customer until we know for certain the transfer failed. Returning it early feels helpful and can result in the money being sent twice.

The app says “sending”, not “sent”, until we are certain. Retracting a success message is far worse than a short wait.

Receiving money from another bank

Money coming in works too — but there is one honest weakness worth understanding, because it is the main thing our team has to engineer around.

When somebody at Access Bank sends ₦50,000 to a BravePay customer, it travels over NIBSS and lands in that customer's account at BankOne. The money arrives correctly and is never at risk.

The problem is that BankOne does not tell us it happened. Most modern systems send an automatic alert the moment something changes. BankOne has no such feature — we reviewed all 78 pages of their documentation and there is nothing of the kind.

How it should work

  • Money arrives.
  • BankOne immediately tells BravePay.
  • Customer gets an instant alert.

How it actually works

  • Money arrives.
  • Silence.
  • BravePay has to keep asking "anything new?"

So we have to poll — keep checking, over and over, for every account. Checking every account every minute would mean well over a million enquiries a day, which is expensive and slow.

The practical answer is to check different accounts at different rates: customers using the app right now get checked every 30 to 60 seconds, recently active customers every ten minutes or so, and everyone else once overnight. That gives fast alerts to the people most likely to be waiting for money, without the cost of checking everyone constantly.

The most valuable question we can ask Qore

Before we build all that, we are asking BankOne directly: is there any way to be told automatically when money arrives? A feed, a file, anything not in the public documentation.

If the answer is yes, a meaningful chunk of engineering work disappears and customers get instant alerts. If the answer is no, we build the checking system described above. Either way it works — but the answer changes the cost, so we want it before we start.

What can go wrong, and what we do

Nothing here is unusual. Every bank deals with the same situations. The difference between a good system and a bad one is whether these were planned for or discovered live.

The transfer is taking a long time

Normal. Something in the chain is slow. We keep asking BankOne for the outcome and keep the customer informed. The money stays held, not returned, until we know.

We never got an answer

We ask again, using exactly the same reference number, so BankOne knows it is the same instruction and does not perform it twice. This is why the reference matters — it is what prevents a repeated question turning into a repeated payment.

The customer taps send twice

The second attempt is recognised as a duplicate and rejected automatically. The money can only leave once.

A payroll run has one bad account

Each employee is treated separately. Thirty-nine people get paid and one is flagged, rather than the whole run failing.

A transfer to another bank needs reversing

Once money has left for another bank, it cannot be pulled back automatically. BankOne is explicit about this, and our systems are built to refuse the attempt. Recovering it becomes a conversation between the two banks — which is exactly why the name-check in step 3 matters so much.

Every day, we check the books agree

Once a day, automatically, we compare our records against BankOne's — every account, every transaction, every balance. Anything that does not match becomes a job for a person to look at, and it is never quietly corrected. This is standard banking practice and it is how problems get found in hours rather than months.

What this means for BravePay

Choosing BankOne is a decision about what we stop building.

BankOne now handles

  • Real account numbers
  • Balances and the official ledger
  • Transfers between banks
  • Cards
  • Loans, fixed deposits, standing orders
  • Freezing accounts and placing holds
  • Monthly reports to the CBN and NDIC

BravePay keeps building

  • The app and everything customers see
  • Login, PIN, fingerprint, devices
  • Invoicing, payroll, expenses, budgets
  • Notifications
  • The admin and support console
  • Fraud rules and spending insights
  • Daily checking of the books

The left-hand column is work we no longer have to do, maintain, or defend to a regulator. The right-hand column is what actually makes BravePay different from every other bank app — and it is entirely ours.

BankOne owns the money. BravePay owns everything that is not money.

Glossary

API
The way two computer systems talk to each other — one asks a question, the other answers.
HTTP / HTTPS
The language of those messages. HTTPS is the sealed, scrambled version. HTTP is the postcard.
NIBSS
Nigeria Inter-Bank Settlement System. The shared national infrastructure that carries money between banks.
NIP
NIBSS Instant Payment. The service that makes bank transfers arrive in seconds.
NUBAN
The standard 10-digit Nigerian account number. Only a licensed core banking system can issue a valid one.
BVN
Bank Verification Number. One identity, shared across all banks, tied to a person's fingerprints.
Core banking system
The engine that holds accounts, balances and the official ledger. Ours is BankOne.
Ledger
The formal record of every movement of money. What an auditor or regulator asks to see.
Reconciliation
Comparing two sets of records to prove they agree. Done daily.
Settlement
The behind-the-scenes squaring-up between banks. Customers see money instantly; the banks settle up separately.
Polling
Repeatedly asking "has anything changed?" because the other system will not tell you on its own.

What we need answered before we build

Three questions to Qore, in writing. Everything else can proceed while we wait.

  1. Is there any way to be told automatically when money arrives? Changes how much we build and how fast customers get alerts.
  2. Is the live connection available in the secure, sealed form? The documentation says otherwise, and we need that in writing before handling real customer data.
  3. Where is our data physically stored? From 1 January 2027, Nigerian payment data has to stay in Nigeria.
